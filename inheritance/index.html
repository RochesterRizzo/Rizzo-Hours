The Inheritance of Western Civilization

What was inherited. What socialism and communism destroyed. How Critical Theory, postmodernism, and identity ideology moved the assault into culture and institutions. What must be defended, recovered, transmitted, and built.

The purpose of this project

This is not a euphemism factory. Ideas have consequences, destructive ideas can migrate from books into institutions, and a civilization that cannot name or transmit its inheritance will eventually lose it.

Seriousness requires more than denunciation. We will preserve sharp arguments, read original texts, put claims against the historical record, trace mechanisms by which ideas become institutional practice, distinguish demonstrated genealogy from asserted genealogy, and ask what ought to be built in place of endless dismantling.

The governing question is not only “What went wrong?” It is also: “What is true, good, beautiful, and worth transmitting—and who will have the courage to transmit it?”

1. Read the originals
Do not argue only with slogans or descendants.
2. Follow the mechanism
A resemblance is not yet a genealogy.
3. Count the consequences
Use archives, testimony, rules, and measurable outcomes.
4. End with construction
Say what should be preserved, repaired, taught, or built.

Map of the project

1. The inheritance itself

Reason, truth, natural law, dignity, constitutional government, property, markets, science, religion, family, duty, beauty, freedom.

2. The socialist and communist promise

Alienation, class conflict, historical materialism, revolution, equality, planning, abolition of private property.

3. The record of communist rule

Revolution, famine, collectivization, terror, camps, surveillance, conformity, civil society, dissident testimony.

4. Frankfurt School and Critical Theory

Instrumental reason, mass culture, authority, family, tolerance, personality, repression, permanent critique.

5. French theory and postmodernism

Power/knowledge, discourse, deconstruction, meaning, anti-foundationalism, subject formation, American reception.

6. Identity, power, and the new orthodoxy

Intersectionality, critical race theory, queer theory, standpoint epistemology, privilege, equity, anti-racism, gender ideology.

7. Transmission through institutions

Universities, schools, foundations, NGOs, media, HR, professional bodies, government, moderation, regulation.

8. Recovery and reconstruction

Truth, moral judgment, ordered liberty, pluralism, free inquiry, family, association, constitutional limits, science, beauty, courage, builders.

Discussion center

Six-question protocol

  1. What is the strongest, most precise version of the claim?
  2. Which primary text or evidence must be true for it to hold?
  3. What mechanism carries the idea into institutions?
  4. Where does evidence end and inference begin?
  5. What is the strongest rival explanation?
  6. What should a builder preserve, reject, repair, or create?

Current Frankfurt file

  • Is Critical Theory permanent negation, or does it contain a positive standard?
  • Does Dialectic indict reason, instrumental reason, capitalist modernity, or domination?
  • When does moral condemnation become psychological diagnosis?
  • Can Marcuse’s tolerance be separated from modern censorship?
  • What would prove or disprove the claimed institutional genealogy?
