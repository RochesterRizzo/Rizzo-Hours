Exemption policy and missed assignments

THREE EXEMPTION POLICY and MISSED Assignments

  • All assignments must be completed unless covered by an exemption.

  • All students are given a NO QUESTIONS ASKED 3 exemptions policy.

    • You do NOT HAVE to use the exemptions

      • You can use 0, 1, 2 or 3

    • But if you are late on an assignment, have a travel conflict, are too sick to get work done, messed up formatting or submissions, can’t dedicate your best efforts for the week, are having personal troubles that week, etc. you MUST take an exemption.

  • There are no extensions, no late work, no make-ups. The exemption policy is built to be the pressure release valve for any conflict.

  • Your RRR grade will be calculated based on all of the work you submit. Your RRR percentage will equal the total points you earn divided by the total points possible on all RRR assignments that you submit and that are not covered by an exemption. An assignment missed after all three exemptions have been used will count as a zero and will also reduce the final weighted course average by two percentage points.

    • So, for example, if each assignment is worth 5 points, and we offer 12 weeks of research assignments and you complete 12 weeks of work and use no exemptions, your research grade will be computed as “total number of research points obtained divided by 60.”

    • If for some reason you used 2 of the 3 exemptions, your grade calculation is “total points scored divided by 50”

    • If you took all 3 exemptions AND missed an additional assignment, your grade calculation is, “total points scored divided by 45” AND THEN we reduce your entire final weighted course average by 2 percentage points.

  • We score every assignment submitted. In other words, you may NOT use your exemptions and ask us to take your “best 9 assignments.” Anything submitted will be counted toward your course grade.

  • We are very likely to be offering one or more “double assignments” which will count as two weeks’ worth of your grade. Skipped double assignments would count as two exemptions.

Homework is intended to drive learning and growth mostly outside of the materials we are covering in lecture itself. Treating homework like a bare-minimum requirement that just needs to be completed reduces not just the assignment, but the course itself, to the bare minimum. Education is supposed to be about so much more than just handing things in. Otherwise, why are you here?

The EXEMPTION program is NOT meant as a way for you to strategically pick which assignments are tougher or not work when you are not in the mood. They are built in as course relief valves in the case of a misstep, mistake, unavoidable last-minute conflict, submission error, late work, family emergency, acute illness. So you are strongly advised to try to leave at least one assignment exemption in reserve in case a mistake appears. The program exists to cover contingencies, not as a strategic grade tool.



Setting Up Your Public Course Portfolio

Setting Up Your Public Course Portfolio

What this is
Starting this semester, your written work for ECON 238 lives on a public site you build and control — not a text box in Blackboard. You'll submit a link to your own page for each assignment. Do this setup now; every assignment sheet going forward assumes it's done.

Part 1 — Build the site (~15 minutes, no coding required)

  1. Create a GitHub account at github.com if you don't already have one. It's free.

  2. Create a new repository.

  • Click the + in the top right → New repository

  • Name it econ238-portfolio

  • Set visibility to Public

  • Check Add a README file

  • Click Create repository

  1. Turn on GitHub Pages.

  • In your new repo, go to Settings → Pages

  • Under "Build and deployment," set Source to Deploy from a branch

  • Set Branch to main, folder to / (root) → Save

  • Wait a minute, refresh — GitHub will show your live URL

  1. Confirm it's live at https://<your-username>.github.io/econ238-portfolio/. Right now it's just showing your README — that's about to become your homepage.

Everything above and everything from here happens in the browser: Add file → Create new file, or the pencil icon on any existing file. No git, no terminal, no command line required. (If you already know git and want to work locally, that's fine — it's the same repo either way.)

Part 2 — Structure

  • README.md is your homepage. GitHub Pages renders it automatically — no extra setup.

  • One markdown file per assignment: assignment-01-<short-topic>.md, assignment-02-<short-topic>.md, etc. Each becomes its own page at its own URL.

  • Link every new assignment file from your README as you add it, so your homepage doubles as a table of contents.

Part 3 — Your README

Replace the default content with something like:

# [Your Name] — ECON 238 Portfolio

Environmental Economics, Fall 2026, University of Rochester

## Assignments

- [Assignment 1: Coase and the ___ dispute](assignment-01-coase.md)

Add one line to that list every time you add a new assignment file. That's the entire homepage-maintenance job.

Part 4 — Submitting work, going forward

For each assignment:

  1. Add or edit the relevant .md file.

  2. Commit the change (the web editor prompts for a commit message — write a real one).

  3. Copy the URL of that specific page, not just your repo's root.

  4. Paste that URL into the Blackboard assignment by the deadline.

Blackboard stays the official deadline and grade record. Your site is where the actual work lives.

A handful of small commits spread over several days reads very differently than one commit at 11:58pm the night it's due. Build the habit of saving as you go.

Part 5 — Name or handle

Real name is the default, and it's the stronger choice — this becomes a portfolio piece, and “here’s my public work from college” is worth more attached to your actual name. If you'd rather not have coursework permanently attached to your legal name in a public search, email me and use a consistent handle instead; I'll map it to you privately. Whichever you pick, keep it the same all semester.

FAQ

Does this cost anything? No. Public repos and GitHub Pages are free.

Can I make it private? No — the point is that it's public. If you have a real concern about that, email me directly rather than working around it on your own.

What happens to it after the semester? It's yours. Keep it, add to it, put it on your resume.

I already have a GitHub account or site from another class. Fine — just add this repo to your existing account.

Who: Rick Hornbeck of the University of Chicago

Professor Hornbeck is the V. Duane Rath Professor of Economics at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business. He is a leading applied microeconomist and economic historian whose research explores the historical development of the American economy. Professor Hornbeck’s research frequently reshapes how we understand major historical shifts and crises.
